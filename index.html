The Republic Against Its Muslims

How laïcité became an instrument of structural Islamophobia in France.

By Aswin Surya & Aarav Gupta

ASAMST 132AC Chicago author-date ≈ 10,000 words April 2026

France hosts the largest Muslim population in Western Europe, an estimated five to six million people, roughly 8 to 9 percent of the national population (Pew Research Center 2017; INSEE-INED 2023). It also operates one of the most elaborate state apparatuses in the democratic world for regulating Muslim religious expression, from the 2004 ban on headscarves in schools to the 2010 ban on full-face veils in public, from the 2021 "anti-separatism law" to the 2023 abaya ban, from the dissolution of France's largest Muslim civil rights organization to ongoing prohibitions on the hijab in football, basketball, and the 2024 Olympics for athletes representing France.

This project advances a single central argument:

Central argument

Islamophobia in France does not operate as scattered prejudice. It functions as a structural project that has weaponized laïcité (a republican secularism originally designed in 1905 to protect citizens from religious coercion) into a state instrument that disciplines Muslim visibility, polices Muslim bodies (especially women's), and constructs Muslims as constitutive outsiders to the Republic. This project is rooted in France's unfinished colonial history, particularly in Algeria, and is now reproduced through a tightly integrated network of legal restrictions, electoral rhetoric, media discourse, surveillance regimes, and economic exclusion.

The dynamic this report examines is precisely what Gema Martín-Muñoz (2010, 21) describes as the European pattern of "a reductionist and monolithic image of 'us' and of 'them' (the two 'cultures')," a frame in which "millions of human beings are designated as 'Western' or 'Muslims' and represent alien and even antagonistic cultures." Drawing on Edward Said's Orientalism (1978) and Covering Islam (1997 [1981]), Hatem Bazian's framework of structural Islamophobia (2018), Mahmood Mamdani's account of how Cold War politics produced the "Good Muslim, Bad Muslim" binary (2004), Martín-Muñoz's analysis of "unconscious Islamophobia" as legitimized self-defense (2010), and Ramón Grosfoguel's analysis of the colonial/modern world-system (Grosfoguel and Mielants 2006), this site examines that structure and the people fighting back against it.
