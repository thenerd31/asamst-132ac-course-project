Section 05

Media, Films, and Images

Cable news, the Bolloré empire, the "experts," and how everyday Muslim life in France is constituted as spectacle.

Edward Said's Covering Islam (1997 [1981]) anticipated the French media pattern by nearly two decades. Writing in the introduction to the 1997 revised edition — fifteen years after the book's first appearance — Said reported that the situation had only worsened:

Coverage was now "characterized by a more highly exaggerated stereotyping and belligerent hostility than what I had previously described in my book. Indeed, Islam's role in hijackings and terrorism, descriptions of the way in which overtly Muslim countries like Iran threaten 'us' and our way of life, and speculations about the latest conspiracy to blow up buildings…has grown to prominence." (Said 1997, xi)

The structural problem he names is the constitution of "Islam" as a single explanatory key for any event involving any Muslim anywhere — a media reflex he calls "essentialist."

The French talk-television circuit is the most concentrated contemporary realization of this pattern. Cable news channels including CNews (owned by Vincent Bolloré, a billionaire who also owns Le Journal du Dimanche) and BFM TV regularly feature panels debating "the Islamic question" with the visible Muslim community largely absent from the panel itself. Zemmour rose to prominence as a commentator on these channels before launching his presidential bid — precisely the trajectory Said warned about, in which "experts" certified by repeated television appearances become the public voice on Muslim matters. The CCIE 2024 report identifies media narratives as one of the three key vectors of structural Islamophobia, alongside policy and discrimination (CCIE 2024).

Said also names the slippage between media discourse and intellectual production: alarming books by figures like Judith Miller and Bernard Lewis "feel secure until Islam puts away its sword," reinforcing public willingness to "accept the unvaryingly reductive arguments about Islam" (Said 1997, xxix). The French equivalents — Caroline Fourest's Frère Tariq, the corpus around the journal Causeur, the sustained presence of Pascal Bruckner and Alain Finkielkraut in opinion pages — perform exactly the function Said diagnoses: lending the prestige of letters to a policy environment that is otherwise embarrassed by its own inconsistencies.

Martín-Muñoz describes the same dynamic from a European vantage. Western media's treatment of the Muslim world, she writes:

Constructs a media discourse at a distance: for example, it almost never portrays individuals, only masses, and usually in relation to violence or fanaticism, implicitly casting doubt on the state of civilization of these peoples. It also presents Islam as the abstract key to explain the absolute evolution of these peoples, portraying an evolving world — "our" world — in contrast to another "Islamic" world condemned to a repetitive cycle of hopeless misery and violence. (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 26)

The 2022 European Islamophobia Report describes how the French government's role consists of "mainstreaming anti-Muslim racism and renewed calls for banning of the hijab in French society" (Bridge Initiative 2023). The same report documents how the 2023 killing of Nahel Merzouk — a 17-year-old French citizen of Algerian descent shot at point-blank range by police during a traffic stop — was framed in dominant media discourse not as a case of police violence against a racialized minority but as a precipitant of "riots in the banlieues," reinforcing the Orientalist trope of the Muslim suburb as inherently unruly (Bayrakli and Hafez 2023).

This is also Mamdani's "Culture Talk" in operation: rather than analyzing the political economy of policing, of housing, of unemployment in the banlieues, French media discourse explains the events as cultural — as the inevitable expression of a Muslim disposition. "Culture Talk after 9/11," Mamdani writes, "qualified and explained the practice of 'terrorism' as 'Islamic'" (Mamdani 2004, 17); French political-media Culture Talk after Nahel qualified and explained protest as banlieue, which is to say as Muslim, which is to say as outside the legitimate citizen body. A particularly stark example came in October 2020 when, days after the murder of Samuel Paty, journalists conducting a televised report on "the rise of Islam in Roubaix" had to be placed under police protection (Times of Israel 2022). The report's premise — that the ordinary practice of Islam in a French city was itself newsworthy — illustrates how quotidian Muslim life is constituted as spectacle.
