Section 02

Research on Islamophobia in France

The empirical signature of structural Islamophobia, and the five analytical lenses — Said, Mamdani, Bazian, Martín-Muñoz, Grosfoguel — that name what the French state would prefer to leave unnamed.

Scale and Trajectory

Quantitative measurement of Islamophobia in France is contested by design — the state's refusal to collect ethno-religious data limits official statistics, and Muslim civil society organizations that produced independent counts (such as the CCIF) were dissolved by government decree in 2020. Despite these obstacles, a converging body of evidence demonstrates that anti-Muslim discrimination is widespread and intensifying.

In 2004 the Council of Europe adopted the working definition Martín-Muñoz cites in her overview of European Islamophobia:

The fear of or prejudiced viewpoint towards Islam, Muslims and matters pertaining to them. Whether it takes the shape of daily forms of racism and discrimination or more violent forms, Islamophobia is a violation of human rights and a threat to social cohesion. (Council of Europe 2004, quoted in Martín-Muñoz 2010, 22)

The French case satisfies both prongs: a sustained pattern of state-mediated discrimination, and a cumulative threat to the civic inclusion of millions of citizens.

Survey evidence. The EU Agency for Fundamental Rights' 2024 Being Muslim in the EU report — the most rigorous cross-national survey to date — found that 47 percent of Muslims across 13 EU states had experienced racial discrimination in the previous five years, up from 39 percent in 2016. In France specifically, 39 percent of Muslims reported discrimination when looking for work, 35 percent in the workplace, and 35 percent could not buy or rent a house because of discrimination — all sharply up from 2016 (FRA 2024). A 2025 IFOP / Grande Mosquée de Paris survey of 1,000 French Muslims found that 66 percent had experienced at least one form of religious discrimination, four times the rate for adherents of other religions, and that 50 percent who had searched for work reported religious discrimination — five times the national rate (IFOP 2025). France's own Defender of Rights reported in late 2025 that 34 percent of Muslims (or those perceived as such) had experienced discrimination, rising to 38 percent for Muslim women (France 24 2025).

Hate-crime evidence. Anti-Muslim acts officially recorded by the French Ministry of the Interior rose 29 percent in 2023 over 2022 (HRW 2025), and reportedly surged 72 percent in the first quarter of 2025 compared to the same period in 2024 (Jacobin 2025; Al Jazeera 2025). The CCIE (the Brussels-successor of the dissolved CCIF) documented 1,037 reports of Islamophobic incidents in 2024 alone — and crucially noted that 80 percent of those reports concerned acts directly linked to government policies or measures: discrimination based on clothing, denial of education or employment due to veiling, abusive administrative searches, heightened mosque surveillance (CCIE 2024).

This is the empirical signature of structural Islamophobia, in the sense Bazian (2015, 2018) describes: not a sum of bigoted individuals but a system of discrimination legitimized by state policy — and, as Martín-Muñoz adds, accompanied by "the sense of denigration and humiliation felt by many Muslim citizens, especially young people, due to the relentless insistence on presenting their faith, culture and identity as inherent sources of decadence, fundamentalism and terrorism" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 23).

Qualitative evidence. A 2010 audit study published in PNAS found that a Muslim job applicant in France is 2.5 times less likely to receive a callback than an otherwise-identical Christian applicant (Adida, Laitin, and Valfort 2010). A more recent field experiment by Valfort (2020) found that religious Muslims must submit twice as many applications as religious Christians to receive a callback, and that "religiosity" functioned as a premium for Christian applicants and a penalty for Muslims.

The Analytical Frame: Said, Mamdani, Bazian, Martín-Muñoz, Grosfoguel

Five analytical lenses from course material structure the rest of this project. Each names something the French state would prefer to leave unnamed.

Edward Said, Covering Islam (1997 [1981]). Said's analysis of how Western media construct "Islam" as a feared and homogenized Other is the indispensable starting point. Writing in the introduction to the revised edition, he observes that in the fifteen years since the book first appeared, coverage had become "characterized by a more highly exaggerated stereotyping and belligerent hostility than what I had previously described in my book" (Said 1997, xi). The deeper problem he identifies is the construction of "Islam" as an undifferentiated essence:

"Islam" defines a relatively small proportion of what actually takes place in the Islamic world, which numbers a billion people, and includes dozens of countries, societies, traditions, languages, and, of course, an infinite number of different experiences. It is simply false to try to trace all this back to something called "Islam." (Said 1997, xvi)

The discourse rests on what Said calls the "slippery concept" of fundamentalism, "a word that has come to be associated almost automatically with Islam" and used to reduce an entire civilization to "a handful of rules, stereotypes, and generalizations about the faith, its founder, and all of its people" (Said 1997, xvi, xviii).

Said is also unsparing about the "experts" — Bernard Lewis, Daniel Pipes, Judith Miller, Martin Peretz — whose Orientalist scholarship and journalism have given respectability to anti-Islamic generalization. Lewis's "extremely tenuous grasp" of contemporary Muslim societies, he writes, is "designed to bolster his views" rather than to describe them; the "essential associations of Islam, for whom in fact the word watan certainly does have the existential associations of unexamined presuppositions" are erased so that Islam can be portrayed as essentially incompatible with modernity (Said 1997, xxx–xxxi). The French intellectual class — Alain Finkielkraut, Pascal Bruckner, Caroline Fourest, Éric Zemmour — occupies the structural position Said describes for Lewis: producing the "expert" rationale by which restrictive policies appear culturally inevitable rather than politically chosen. As Said summarizes the underlying premise of this discourse, the West and Islam are imagined as "shut off from each other, despite literally centuries of borrowing and crossing over which Lewis totally denies" (Said 1997, xxxiii). This is the rhetoric on which the laïcité apparatus depends: France against Islam, rather than France with its Muslim citizens.

Mahmood Mamdani, Good Muslim, Bad Muslim (2004). Mamdani extends Said's discursive analysis into a structural account of how Cold War and post-9/11 politics manufactured Muslim political identity into a binary. He gives this binary its sharpest formulation in his introduction:

From this point of view, "bad Muslims" were clearly responsible for terrorism. At the same time, the president seemed to assure Americans that "good Muslims" were anxious to clear their names and consciences of this horrible crime and would undoubtedly support "us" in a war against "them." (Mamdani 2004, 15)

Crucially, the binary is political, not theological: "Judgments of 'good' and 'bad' refer to Muslim political identities, not to cultural or religious ones" (Mamdani 2004, 15). The visibly devout Muslim is not "bad" because of doctrine; she is "bad" because her visibility refuses the political demand for invisibility.

Mamdani's broader frame is what he calls "Culture Talk" — the post–Cold War habit of explaining political conflicts in cultural terms. "Culture Talk," he writes, "assumes that every culture has a tangible essence that defines it, and it then explains politics as a consequence of that essence" (Mamdani 2004, 17). It treats culture as monolithic, determinist, and prior to action: "Unlike the culture studied by anthropologists — face-to-face, intimate, local, and lived — the talk of culture is highly politicized and comes in large geo-packages" (Mamdani 2004, 17). When France's interior ministers describe Muslim civic associations as "Islamist," when President Macron speaks of "Islamist separatism" rather than political grievance, when journalists discuss "the rise of Islam in Roubaix" rather than ordinary religious practice by French citizens, this is Culture Talk in operation. It transforms political problems (housing, employment, policing, foreign policy) into civilizational ones — which is to say, into problems that admit no political solution, only containment of the offending culture.

Mamdani also locates the Muslim predicament within modernity itself. Reflecting on Hannah Arendt and Frantz Fanon, he argues that political violence in the modern era is not a deviation from progress but constitutive of it:

Such violence gets discussed in two basic ways: in cultural terms for a premodern society and theological terms for a modern society. The cultural explanation always attributes political violence to the absence of modernity. (Mamdani 2004, 4)

What modernity tends to ignore is that the "uniqueness of the Holocaust" sits at "the meeting point of two traditions that marked modern Western civilization: 'the anti-Semitic tradition and the tradition of genocide of colonized peoples'" (Mamdani 2004, 7, quoting Lindqvist). The French structural Islamophobia analyzed in this project is the converse of that pattern: it produces the boundary between "the West" and "the rest" internally, by manufacturing the French Muslim as a cultural Other within the citizen body.

Hatem Bazian's framework of structural Islamophobia (Bazian 2015, 2018) extends Said and Mamdani into an explicitly structural register. Bazian argues that contemporary Islamophobia operates as a structural organizing principle — not as scattered hatred but as the rationalized maintenance of a global racial-civilizational hierarchy. The French case fits the model precisely: a centralized state deploys legal, surveillance, educational, and economic instruments simultaneously, all of them justified by appeals to universal republican values that are nonetheless applied selectively to one community. As Bazian (2018) frames it in his work on the Clash of Civilizations, Islamophobia constructs a singular and homogenous undifferentiated image of Muslim men and women as religious fanatics, antithetical to civilization itself — the same image Said diagnosed in the Western media and Mamdani diagnosed in the policy class.

Gema Martín-Muñoz's "Unconscious Islamophobia" (2010) is especially valuable for the French case because it is written from within the European context and names the precise dynamic the laïcité apparatus exploits. Martín-Muñoz argues that:

Since these sentiments are expressions of patriotism and self-defence, the ensuing Islamophobic sentiments are legitimised and forgiven by society. Hence, the term "unconscious Islamophobia" would be correct if that is understood to mean protection and self-defence instead of discrimination. For this reason, many are reluctant to call it by its name. (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27)

This is the ideological move that distinguishes contemporary French political Islamophobia from openly far-right xenophobia: it claims to defend not whiteness or Christianity but the Republic itself — its values, its women, its laïcité.

Martín-Muñoz also identifies the mechanism by which this "unconscious" version provides cover for the explicit version:

Extreme right-wing parties that are consolidating their positions in different European countries have generally evolved from xenophobic positions to promote an explicitly anti-Muslim discourse. They use this discourse to fuel Islamophobic sentiment and at the same time, and in contrast to xenophobia in general, present this discourse with greater social legitimisation, taking advantage of unconscious Islamophobia. (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27)

This is precisely what Marine Le Pen's dédiabolisation strategy has accomplished — and what Macron's centrist drift has institutionalized into law.

Martín-Muñoz also names the same Mamdani binary from a European vantage point. There is, she writes:

The tendency to distinguish between "good Muslims" and "bad Muslims." The former would be Westernized Muslims who declare themselves to be secular and often, without having scientific qualifications, confirm our demonized stereotypes of Islam and its alleged "diseases" …If the rest do not prove that they are "good" Muslims, they are considered "bad" Muslims. (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 28)

The French state's Forum de l'Islam de France — established to construct an officially-approved "French Islam" — is the institutional realization of exactly this distinction.

Ramón Grosfoguel's colonial/modern world-system analysis (Grosfoguel and Mielants 2006) provides the historical depth: the racialization of Muslims in France today is the continuation of the colonial racial order that governed the French Empire from 1830 to 1962. The same population that France ruled as colonial subjects in Algeria is now governed as a racialized internal Other in the metropole. This is the longue durée within which the laïcité apparatus must be situated.
