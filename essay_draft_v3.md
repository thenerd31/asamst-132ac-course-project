## LANDING PAGE / PROJECT INTRODUCTION

France hosts the largest Muslim population in Western Europe — an estimated five to six million people, roughly 8–9 percent of the national population (Pew Research Center 2017; INSEE-INED 2023). It also operates one of the most elaborate state apparatuses in the democratic world for regulating Muslim religious expression, from the 2004 ban on headscarves in schools to the 2010 ban on full-face veils in public, from the 2021 "anti-separatism law" to the 2023 abaya ban, from the dissolution of France's largest Muslim civil rights organization to ongoing prohibitions on the hijab in football, basketball, and the 2024 Olympics for athletes representing France.

This project advances a single central argument:

> **Islamophobia in France does not operate as scattered prejudice. It functions as a structural project that has weaponized *laïcité* — a republican secularism originally designed in 1905 to protect citizens from religious coercion — into a state instrument that disciplines Muslim visibility, polices Muslim bodies (especially women's), and constructs Muslims as constitutive outsiders to the Republic. This project is rooted in France's unfinished colonial history, particularly in Algeria, and is now reproduced through a tightly integrated network of legal restrictions, electoral rhetoric, media discourse, surveillance regimes, and economic exclusion.**

The dynamic this report examines is precisely what Gema Martín-Muñoz (2010, 21) describes as the European pattern of "a reductionist and monolithic image of 'us' and of 'them' (the two 'cultures')," a frame in which "millions of human beings are designated as 'Western' or 'Muslims' and represent alien and even antagonistic cultures." Drawing on Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and *Covering Islam* (1997 [1981]), Hatem Bazian's framework of structural Islamophobia (2018), Mahmood Mamdani's account of how Cold War politics produced the "Good Muslim, Bad Muslim" binary (2004), Martin-Muñoz's analysis of "unconscious Islamophobia" as legitimized self-defense (2010), and Ramón Grosfoguel's analysis of the colonial/modern world-system (Grosfoguel and Mielants 2006), this site examines that structure and the people fighting back against it.

## SECTION 1 — COUNTRY PROFILE

### Geography, Population, Religious Diversity

France is a republic of approximately 68.4 million people (CIA World Factbook 2024). Officially, the French state does not collect data on religion or ethnicity in its census; the 1872 law prohibits religious or ethnic categorization, a fact that itself reflects the universalist republican ideology that shapes everything else in this report. Estimates of the Muslim population therefore rely on survey research and demographic modeling.

Pew Research Center (2017) estimated approximately 5.7 million Muslims in France — the second-largest Muslim community in Western Europe after Germany — comprising roughly 8.8 percent of the population. The 2019–2020 INSEE-INED *Trajectories and Origins* survey (the second of its kind) provides the most rigorous recent demographic profile of French Muslims (INSEE-INED 2023).

The community is overwhelmingly Maghrebi in origin, with the remainder drawn primarily from sub-Saharan Africa and Turkey — a distribution that mirrors France's twentieth-century colonial presence (Pew 2017; Brookings Institution 2007). Critically, **more than half of French Muslims are French-born citizens**: roughly 50 percent were born French, 24 percent acquired citizenship, and 26 percent are foreign nationals (Pew 2017). This distribution matters because much of French political rhetoric speaks of "Muslims" as if they were recent or foreign, when most are second- or third-generation citizens whose presence in France is a direct legacy of French colonialism.

### Historical Roots: From Empire to Banlieue

Muslim presence in France is inseparable from French imperial history. France conquered Algeria in 1830, Tunisia in 1881, and Morocco in 1912; it ruled West African territories with majority-Muslim populations from the 1880s and held a mandate over Syria and Lebanon from 1920 (Brookings Institution 2007). During the First World War, approximately 500,000 African soldiers — the majority of them Muslim from the Maghreb and West Africa — fought under the French flag in mainland France; an estimated 100,000 died, including roughly 70,000 at Verdun alone (Modern Ghana / RFI 2026). The first stone of the Great Mosque of Paris was laid in 1922 and the building was inaugurated in 1926 to honor those soldiers — funded in part by the French state through the law of 19 August 1920, in a striking exception to the 1905 separation of Churches and State (Modern Ghana / RFI 2026).

The contemporary Muslim community, however, was largely formed by post-1945 labor migration. Following the Algerian War of Independence (1954–1962), France actively recruited North African workers to fill labor shortages in the industrial economy. After the 1973 oil crisis ended large-scale labor migration, family reunification became the dominant pathway, and the postwar generation gave rise to the *banlieues* — the often impoverished suburban housing developments where much of the French Muslim population still lives today (Brookings Institution 2007).

This colonial-to-postcolonial trajectory matters analytically. As Grosfoguel and Mielants (2006) argue from the world-systems perspective, the racial categorization of Muslims in Europe cannot be separated from the longue durée of the colonial/modern capitalist/patriarchal world-system — the same epistemic structure that legitimated French rule in Algeria continues to legitimate the marginalization of Algerians' descendants in France. Martín-Muñoz puts the longer European arc bluntly: "European colonial thought felt that it had to provide moral and ethical justification for the political domination and economic exploitation it exerted beyond its borders. This gave rise to a duality between 'civilisation' and 'barbarism,' the concept of race and the principle of European cultural superiority over 'other cultures'" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 24). She quotes the French statesman Jules Ferry's 1885 parliamentary speech invoking the duty "of superior races to civilize inferior races" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 24). The vocabulary of contemporary French universalism — *valeurs républicaines*, *intégration*, *vivre-ensemble* — has substituted civilizational uplift for biological race, but the structural relation between metropole and (former) colonial subject is recognizably continuous. As one researcher noted regarding the 2021 separatism law, "Accusing some Muslims today of being separatists is therefore part of France's revolutionary and colonial heritage. It implies that a part of the national community behaves like an enemy within" (The Conversation 2021).

### What is Laïcité?

To understand French Islamophobia, one must understand *laïcité*, the French principle of secularism enshrined in the law of 9 December 1905 on the Separation of the Churches and the State. The 1905 law was originally a liberal settlement of the conflict between the French Republic and the Catholic Church. It rests on three principles: independence of the state from religion, freedom of conscience, and non-discrimination based on religion (Cesari 2023).

The 1905 law, in its original form, was *protective* of religious minorities — it removed state coercion and guaranteed each citizen the right to practice or not practice any faith. As French legal scholar Rim-Sarah Alouane told CNN in 2023, contemporary policy "stands in opposition to the liberal core of the 1905 Law on Separation of Church & State — a law we've been distorting and weaponizing since the '90s" (CNN 2023).

This is the analytical hinge of the project: France did not invent a new anti-Muslim ideology from scratch. It took a liberal protection and inverted it into an instrument of religious surveillance, applied with disproportionate force to one community.

## SECTION 2 — RESEARCH ON ISLAMOPHOBIA IN FRANCE

### Scale and Trajectory

Quantitative measurement of Islamophobia in France is contested by design — the state's refusal to collect ethno-religious data limits official statistics, and Muslim civil society organizations that produced independent counts (such as the CCIF) were dissolved by government decree in 2020. Despite these obstacles, a converging body of evidence demonstrates that anti-Muslim discrimination is widespread and intensifying.

In 2004 the Council of Europe adopted the working definition Martín-Muñoz cites in her overview of European Islamophobia: it is "the fear of or prejudiced viewpoint towards Islam, Muslims and matters pertaining to them. Whether it takes the shape of daily forms of racism and discrimination or more violent forms, Islamophobia is a violation of human rights and a threat to social cohesion" (Council of Europe 2004, quoted in Martín-Muñoz 2010, 22). The French case satisfies both prongs: a sustained pattern of state-mediated discrimination, and a cumulative threat to the civic inclusion of millions of citizens.

**Survey evidence.** The EU Agency for Fundamental Rights' 2024 *Being Muslim in the EU* report — the most rigorous cross-national survey to date — found that 47 percent of Muslims across 13 EU states had experienced racial discrimination in the previous five years, up from 39 percent in 2016. In France specifically, 39 percent of Muslims reported discrimination when looking for work, 35 percent in the workplace, and 35 percent could not buy or rent a house because of discrimination — all sharply up from 2016 (FRA 2024). A 2025 IFOP / Grande Mosquée de Paris survey of 1,000 French Muslims found that 66 percent had experienced at least one form of religious discrimination, four times the rate for adherents of other religions, and that 50 percent who had searched for work reported religious discrimination — five times the national rate (IFOP 2025). France's own Defender of Rights reported in late 2025 that 34 percent of Muslims (or those perceived as such) had experienced discrimination, rising to 38 percent for Muslim women (France 24 2025).

**Hate-crime evidence.** Anti-Muslim acts officially recorded by the French Ministry of the Interior rose 29 percent in 2023 over 2022 (HRW 2025), and reportedly surged 72 percent in the first quarter of 2025 compared to the same period in 2024 (Jacobin 2025; Al Jazeera 2025). The CCIE (the Brussels-successor of the dissolved CCIF) documented 1,037 reports of Islamophobic incidents in 2024 alone — and crucially noted that **80 percent of those reports concerned acts directly linked to government policies or measures**: discrimination based on clothing, denial of education or employment due to veiling, abusive administrative searches, heightened mosque surveillance (CCIE 2024).

This is the empirical signature of *structural* Islamophobia, in the sense Bazian (2015, 2018) describes: not a sum of bigoted individuals but a system of discrimination legitimized by state policy — and, as Martín-Muñoz adds, accompanied by "the sense of denigration and humiliation felt by many Muslim citizens, especially young people, due to the relentless insistence on presenting their faith, culture and identity as inherent sources of decadence, fundamentalism and terrorism" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 23).

**Qualitative evidence.** A 2010 audit study published in *PNAS* found that a Muslim job applicant in France is **2.5 times less likely** to receive a callback than an otherwise-identical Christian applicant (Adida, Laitin, and Valfort 2010). A more recent field experiment by Valfort (2020) found that religious Muslims must submit twice as many applications as religious Christians to receive a callback, and that "religiosity" functioned as a *premium* for Christian applicants and a *penalty* for Muslims.

### The Analytical Frame: Said, Mamdani, Bazian, Martín-Muñoz, Grosfoguel

Five analytical lenses from course material structure the rest of this project. Each names something the French state would prefer to leave unnamed.

**Edward Said, *Covering Islam* (1997 [1981]).** Said's analysis of how Western media construct "Islam" as a feared and homogenized Other is the indispensable starting point. Writing in the introduction to the revised edition, he observes that in the fifteen years since the book first appeared, coverage had become "characterized by a more highly exaggerated stereotyping and belligerent hostility than what I had previously described in my book" (Said 1997, xi). The deeper problem he identifies is the construction of "Islam" as an undifferentiated essence: "'Islam' defines a relatively small proportion of what actually takes place in the Islamic world, which numbers a billion people, and includes dozens of countries, societies, traditions, languages, and, of course, an infinite number of different experiences. It is simply false to try to trace all this back to something called 'Islam'" (Said 1997, xvi). The discourse rests on what Said calls the "slippery concept" of *fundamentalism*, "a word that has come to be associated almost automatically with Islam" and used to reduce an entire civilization to "a handful of rules, stereotypes, and generalizations about the faith, its founder, and all of its people" (Said 1997, xvi, xviii).

Said is also unsparing about the "experts" — Bernard Lewis, Daniel Pipes, Judith Miller, Martin Peretz — whose Orientalist scholarship and journalism have given respectability to anti-Islamic generalization. Lewis's "extremely tenuous grasp" of contemporary Muslim societies, he writes, is "designed to bolster his views" rather than to describe them; the "essential associations of Islam, for whom in fact the word *watan* certainly does have the existential associations of unexamined presuppositions" are erased so that Islam can be portrayed as essentially incompatible with modernity (Said 1997, xxx–xxxi). The French intellectual class — Alain Finkielkraut, Pascal Bruckner, Caroline Fourest, Éric Zemmour — occupies the structural position Said describes for Lewis: producing the "expert" rationale by which restrictive policies appear culturally inevitable rather than politically chosen. As Said summarizes the underlying premise of this discourse, the West and Islam are imagined as "shut off from each other, despite literally centuries of borrowing and crossing over which Lewis totally denies" (Said 1997, xxxiii). This is the rhetoric on which the laïcité apparatus depends: France against Islam, rather than France with its Muslim citizens.

**Mahmood Mamdani, *Good Muslim, Bad Muslim* (2004).** Mamdani extends Said's discursive analysis into a structural account of how Cold War and post-9/11 politics manufactured Muslim political identity into a binary. He gives this binary its sharpest formulation in his introduction: "From this point of view, 'bad Muslims' were clearly responsible for terrorism. At the same time, the president seemed to assure Americans that 'good Muslims' were anxious to clear their names and consciences of this horrible crime and would undoubtedly support 'us' in a war against 'them'" (Mamdani 2004, 15). Crucially, the binary is *political*, not theological: "Judgments of 'good' and 'bad' refer to Muslim political identities, not to cultural or religious ones" (Mamdani 2004, 15). The visibly devout Muslim is not "bad" because of doctrine; she is "bad" because her visibility refuses the political demand for invisibility.

Mamdani's broader frame is what he calls "Culture Talk" — the post–Cold War habit of explaining political conflicts in cultural terms. "Culture Talk," he writes, "assumes that every culture has a tangible essence that defines it, and it then explains politics as a consequence of that essence" (Mamdani 2004, 17). It treats culture as monolithic, determinist, and prior to action: "Unlike the culture studied by anthropologists — face-to-face, intimate, local, and lived — the talk of culture is highly politicized and comes in large geo-packages" (Mamdani 2004, 17). When France's interior ministers describe Muslim civic associations as "Islamist," when President Macron speaks of "Islamist separatism" rather than political grievance, when journalists discuss "the rise of Islam in Roubaix" rather than ordinary religious practice by French citizens, this is Culture Talk in operation. It transforms political problems (housing, employment, policing, foreign policy) into civilizational ones — which is to say, into problems that admit no political solution, only containment of the offending culture.

Mamdani also locates the Muslim predicament within modernity itself. Reflecting on Hannah Arendt and Frantz Fanon, he argues that political violence in the modern era is not a deviation from progress but constitutive of it: "Such violence gets discussed in two basic ways: in cultural terms for a premodern society and theological terms for a modern society. The cultural explanation always attributes political violence to the absence of modernity" (Mamdani 2004, 4). What modernity tends to ignore is that the "uniqueness of the Holocaust" sits at "the meeting point of two traditions that marked modern Western civilization: 'the anti-Semitic tradition and the tradition of genocide of colonized peoples'" (Mamdani 2004, 7, quoting Lindqvist). The French structural Islamophobia analyzed in this project is the converse of that pattern: it produces the boundary between "the West" and "the rest" *internally*, by manufacturing the French Muslim as a cultural Other within the citizen body.

**Hatem Bazian's framework of structural Islamophobia** (Bazian 2015, 2018) extends Said and Mamdani into an explicitly structural register. Bazian argues that contemporary Islamophobia operates as a structural organizing principle — not as scattered hatred but as the rationalized maintenance of a global racial-civilizational hierarchy. The French case fits the model precisely: a centralized state deploys legal, surveillance, educational, and economic instruments simultaneously, all of them justified by appeals to universal republican values that are nonetheless applied selectively to one community. As Bazian (2018) frames it in his work on the Clash of Civilizations, Islamophobia constructs a singular and homogenous undifferentiated image of Muslim men and women as religious fanatics, antithetical to civilization itself — the same image Said diagnosed in the Western media and Mamdani diagnosed in the policy class.

**Gema Martín-Muñoz's "Unconscious Islamophobia" (2010)** is especially valuable for the French case because it is written from within the European context and names the precise dynamic the laïcité apparatus exploits. Martín-Muñoz argues that "since these sentiments are expressions of patriotism and self-defence, the ensuing Islamophobic sentiments are legitimised and forgiven by society. Hence, the term 'unconscious Islamophobia' would be correct if that is understood to mean protection and self-defence instead of discrimination. For this reason, many are reluctant to call it by its name" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27). This is the ideological move that distinguishes contemporary French political Islamophobia from openly far-right xenophobia: it claims to defend not whiteness or Christianity but the Republic itself — its values, its women, its laïcité.

Martín-Muñoz also identifies the mechanism by which this "unconscious" version provides cover for the explicit version: "Extreme right-wing parties that are consolidating their positions in different European countries have generally evolved from xenophobic positions to promote an explicitly anti-Muslim discourse. They use this discourse to fuel Islamophobic sentiment and at the same time, and in contrast to xenophobia in general, present this discourse with greater social legitimisation, taking advantage of unconscious Islamophobia" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27). This is precisely what Marine Le Pen's *dédiabolisation* strategy has accomplished — and what Macron's centrist drift has institutionalized into law.

Martín-Muñoz also names the same Mamdani binary from a European vantage point: there is, she writes, "the tendency to distinguish between 'good Muslims' and 'bad Muslims.' The former would be Westernized Muslims who declare themselves to be secular and often, without having scientific qualifications, confirm our demonized stereotypes of Islam and its alleged 'diseases' …If the rest do not prove that they are 'good' Muslims, they are considered 'bad' Muslims" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 28). The French state's *Forum de l'Islam de France* — established to construct an officially-approved "French Islam" — is the institutional realization of exactly this distinction.

**Ramón Grosfoguel's colonial/modern world-system analysis** (Grosfoguel and Mielants 2006) provides the historical depth: the racialization of Muslims in France today is the continuation of the colonial racial order that governed the French Empire from 1830 to 1962. The same population that France ruled as colonial subjects in Algeria is now governed as a racialized internal Other in the metropole. This is the longue durée within which the laïcité apparatus must be situated.

## SECTION 3 — LEGAL RESTRICTIONS

France has built, over two decades, the most elaborate legal architecture of religious restriction in Western Europe. Each law was framed in universal terms — applying to "all conspicuous religious symbols" or "all face coverings" — but each was politically motivated by, debated in terms of, and applied disproportionately to, Islamic religious practice.

### The 2004 Headscarf Ban (Loi du 15 mars 2004)

Law no. 2004-228 prohibits the wearing of "conspicuous" religious symbols in public primary and secondary schools. While formally applying to large Christian crosses, Jewish kippahs, and Sikh turbans, the political debate that produced the law was entirely about the Muslim headscarf, following two decades of controversy that began with the 1989 Creil affair, when three girls were expelled for wearing headscarves to school.

### The 2010 Full-Face Veil Ban (Loi du 11 octobre 2010)

Law no. 2010-1192 prohibits face covering in public space. Wearing the niqab or burqa in any public area — street, shop, public transport — is punishable by a €150 fine. A French citizen of Pakistani origin challenged the law at the European Court of Human Rights in *S.A.S. v. France* (2014); the court upheld the ban by 15 votes to 2, justifying it on the grounds of "le vivre-ensemble" (living together), even while noting that "a state which enters into a legislative process of this kind takes the risk of contributing to the consolidation of the stereotypes which affect certain categories of the population and of encouraging the expression of intolerance" (S.A.S. v. France 2014, paragraph 149). In 2018, the UN Human Rights Committee found that the ban violated the human rights of those who wore the niqab (CNN 2023).

### The 2021 Separatism Law (Loi confortant le respect des principes de la République)

Law no. 2021-1109, promulgated 24 August 2021, was Macron's flagship legislative response to the 2020 murder of teacher Samuel Paty. Officially "reinforcing respect for the principles of the Republic," it was understood by both supporters and opponents as targeting "Islamist separatism" — Macron's own framing in his October 2020 Les Mureaux speech. Its provisions:

- Subject religious associations to government review of their bank accounts and require declaration of foreign donations
- Allow administrative dissolution of associations deemed to "encourage hate"
- Extend the principle of *neutralité* (banning religious symbols) from civil servants to all private contractors providing public services — meaning, for instance, that a Muslim employee of a contracted bus company can no longer wear a hijab at work
- Require associations receiving public subsidies to sign a "contract of republican engagement"
- Require government authorization for home schooling
- Criminalize the issuance of "virginity certificates" (a measure framed as women's rights)
- Refuse residency to any foreigner found to be in a polygamous arrangement
- Create a new criminal offense of online hate speech endangering public officials

One year after adoption, the law had led to the closure of more than **800 establishments**, including mosques, prayer rooms, private schools, cultural associations, and restaurants (Melbourne Asia Review 2023). Between 2018 and 2020 — even before the law was passed — the French state had already shut down 672 Muslim-run entities (Al Jazeera 2025).

The political scientist Jocelyne Cesari has analyzed this trajectory as "securitisation," which she defines as the range of "political actions targeting Muslims within the bounds of regular political procedures not directly relevant to the prevention of terrorism" (Cesari 2023). The law's own name is revealing: by framing the legitimate practice of Islam as potential "separatism," it inscribes Muslim difference itself as a threat to republican unity.

### The 2023 Abaya Ban

On 27 August 2023, Education Minister Gabriel Attal announced that the abaya — a long, loose dress worn by some Muslim women — would be prohibited in public schools, citing *laïcité* and arguing that "you must not be able to identify the religious identity of students just by looking at them" (CNN 2023). Importantly, the abaya is not in itself religious garb — it is a cultural garment that can be worn by anyone and that Muslims themselves describe as traditional rather than devotional (Al Jazeera 2023a). The Council of State upheld the ban on 7 September 2023, asserting that the wearing of the abaya "follows the logic of religious affirmation" (Al Jazeera 2023b). Amnesty International condemned the ban as a violation of France's international human rights obligations and called for its repeal (Amnesty International 2023).

On the first day of the 2023 school year, 298 girls wore abayas to school; 67 refused to remove them and were sent home (CNN 2023).

What the abaya ban demonstrates with unusual clarity is the operation of Mamdani's Culture Talk at the level of administrative decree: the state is empowered to read religious meaning into cultural garments, then ban them on the basis of the reading it has imposed. Martín-Muñoz observes that the analogous European debate about freedom of expression and the cartoons of Mohammed could not be reduced to an abstract question of free speech because "what converted the publication of the cartoons in the Danish newspaper *Jyllands-Posten* into a powder-keg situation was the Islamophobic nature and incitement of hatred deriving from the portrayal of the founder of Islam as a terrorist. The nature of the message was clear: if the founder of Islam was a terrorist then all its members are terrorists" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27). The structural mirror image operates in the abaya case: the state defines a garment as religious so that it can define the wearer as a transgressor of secular order. Both cases convert a single cultural sign into a verdict on a billion people.

### Sports Bans

France's Football Federation (FFF) has banned the hijab in football since 2006; the basketball federation followed in 2022 and the volleyball federation in 2023 (ABC News 2024). In June 2023 France's Council of State upheld the FFF ban, accepting the federation's argument that prohibition was justified to "avoid clashes or confrontation" — a reasoning Amnesty International characterized as "extremely problematic" because it implicitly justifies discrimination against the victim of potential discrimination (Amnesty International 2024).

In the run-up to the 2024 Paris Olympics — promoted as the first "gender-equal" Games — the French Sports Ministry confirmed that French athletes representing France could not wear the hijab in competition, on the grounds that they are considered "civil servants" subject to *laïcité* (Time 2024). French sprinter Sounkamba Sylla revealed shortly before the Games that she would not be allowed to participate in the Opening Ceremony because of her headscarf (ABC News 2024). Amnesty International concluded that the policy "breaches international human rights treaties to which France is a party" (Jurist 2024). Notably, athletes representing other countries faced no such restriction at the Paris Games — meaning France's host policy applied a stricter standard to its own Muslim citizens than to foreign visitors.

### Immigration and Refugee Policy

France's restrictions extend to non-citizens. The 2021 law refuses residency permits to applicants found to be in polygamous arrangements (a status France itself does not recognize but which it polices retroactively in immigration files). Beginning under Sarkozy's interior ministry and continuing under successive governments, France has heavily securitized asylum processing for applicants from Muslim-majority countries. Human Rights Watch's 2025 World Report documents how the broader policy environment toward Muslim immigration has hardened in tandem with the domestic *laïcité* apparatus, with restrictive asylum reforms and intensified administrative scrutiny of associations linked to Muslim immigrant communities (HRW 2025).

## SECTION 4 — POLITICAL FIGURES & ELECTORAL ISLAMOPHOBIA

If the legal architecture is the skeleton of structural Islamophobia, electoral politics is the muscle that drives its expansion. Three figures illustrate the continuum.

### Marine Le Pen (Rassemblement National, formerly Front National)

Le Pen took over the Front National from her father Jean-Marie Le Pen in 2011 and rebranded it the Rassemblement National (RN) in 2018, pursuing a strategy of *dédiabolisation* — making the party's ideology palatable to mainstream voters. In substance, however, RN's anti-Muslim positioning has only intensified. In a January 2021 press conference launching the policy that anchored her 2022 presidential campaign, Le Pen called for **a ban on the hijab in all public places**, declaring that "the headscarf is an Islamist item of clothing" and that the broader "Islamist ideologies" she sought to outlaw were "totalitarian and murderous" (Al Jazeera 2021). She advanced to the second round and received 41.5 percent of the vote against Macron — a record for the French far right. In June 2024, RN became the largest French delegation to the European Parliament, prompting Macron to call snap legislative elections marked by what Human Rights Watch described as "increased racist and xenophobic rhetoric" (HRW 2025).

Le Pen's success illustrates the precise mechanism Martín-Muñoz identifies (2010, 27): the migration of explicit anti-Muslim discourse from the xenophobic margins to mainstream legitimacy by trading on a society-wide "unconscious Islamophobia" that has already redefined anti-Muslim measures as patriotic self-defense. The party did not need to soften its program — it needed only to frame the program in republican rather than racial terms. *Laïcité* did the rest of the work.

### Éric Zemmour (Reconquête)

A polemicist and television commentator turned politician, Zemmour ran for the presidency in 2022 on an explicitly civilizational platform, openly endorsing the "Great Replacement" conspiracy theory — the white-nationalist claim that Europe's white Christian population is being deliberately replaced by Muslim immigrants. He has described French banlieues as sites of "jihad" and called for a Ministry of Remigration to deport Muslims en masse (INFORRM 2023). The European Court of Human Rights case *Zemmour v. France* (2022) records his on-camera statements that "for thirty years we've been experiencing an invasion, a colonisation, which is bringing about a conflagration" and that Muslims in France "need to be given a choice between Islam and France" (ECtHR 2022, para. 6, citing France 5 broadcast of 16 September 2016). The Cour de cassation made his conviction for incitement to discrimination and religious hatred definitive on 17 September 2019, ruling that the statements "designated all Muslims in France as invaders and ordered them to renounce their religion or leave the territory of the Republic" (Cour de cassation 2019, para. 12). He finished fourth in the 2022 first round with 7 percent of the vote — a remarkable showing for a candidate openly advocating mass deportation — and the European Court of Human Rights rejected his free-speech appeal in December 2022 (ECtHR 2022).

Zemmour is the French exemplar of what Said diagnosed in the figure of Bernard Lewis: the credentialed expert whose function is to lend intellectual respectability to the proposition that Islam is essentially incompatible with the West. Lewis, Said wrote, marshaled a distorted erudition designed "to portray the whole of Islam as basically *outside* the known, familiar, acceptable world that 'we' inhabit" (Said 1997, xxx). Zemmour's invocations of medieval French history and demographic data perform exactly the same operation in the French setting — and have moved the Overton window far enough that openly mass-deportationist positions are now within the mainstream electoral conversation.

### Emmanuel Macron and the "Centrist" Drift

The most analytically important figure is not Le Pen or Zemmour but Emmanuel Macron, because Macron demonstrates how Islamophobic frames have migrated from the far right to the center and become governing policy. Macron campaigned in 2017 as a centrist liberal; by his second term, his interior minister Gérald Darmanin was attacking Marine Le Pen from her right, accusing her of being "too soft" on Islam (Al Jazeera 2025). In October 2020 Macron delivered a major speech announcing a campaign against "Islamist separatism," which became the framing of the 2021 law. As Al Jazeera's analysis puts it, "in the late 2010s [Islamophobia] entered the mainstream by being embraced by centrist politicians and the media" (Al Jazeera 2025).

This is precisely the dynamic Bazian (2018) identifies: the "Clash of Civilizations" frame, originally a niche position of conservative theorists like Huntington, has been adopted by the centrist political class as the operating logic of governance. It is also the institutional realization of the Mamdani binary. By creating the *Forum de l'Islam de France* in 2022 to construct an officially-approved "French Islam," Macron's government has effectively built an apparatus for producing what Mamdani calls "good Muslims" — those whose political identity has been certified compatible with the state (Mamdani 2004, 15) — and then dissolving the organizations of those who have not received the certification. Martín-Muñoz's gloss of the same dichotomy fits the French setting word for word: the certified "good Muslim" in the European imagination is "Westernized," "secular," and willing to "confirm our demonized stereotypes of Islam"; the rest are reclassified as "bad" by default (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 28).

### Gérald Darmanin and Bruno Retailleau

Darmanin (Interior Minister 2020–2024, now Justice Minister) and his successor Bruno Retailleau (Interior Minister from 2024) have been the executive face of this turn. Darmanin ordered the dissolution of CCIF in 2020. Retailleau, in a *Le Figaro* interview reported by Jacobin, explicitly rejected the term "Islamophobia," claiming it has "an ideological connotation because it comes from the Muslim Brotherhood" (Jacobin 2025), and at a March 2025 rally titled "For the Republic, Against Islamism" he told attendees: "Down with the veil" (Jacobin 2025). Both are now positioning for the 2027 presidency.

## SECTION 5 — MEDIA, FILMS, AND IMAGES

Edward Said's *Covering Islam* (1997 [1981]) anticipated the French media pattern by nearly two decades. Writing in the introduction to the 1997 revised edition — fifteen years after the book's first appearance — Said reported that the situation had only worsened: coverage was now "characterized by a more highly exaggerated stereotyping and belligerent hostility than what I had previously described in my book. Indeed, Islam's role in hijackings and terrorism, descriptions of the way in which overtly Muslim countries like Iran threaten 'us' and our way of life, and speculations about the latest conspiracy to blow up buildings…has grown to prominence" (Said 1997, xi). The structural problem he names is the constitution of "Islam" as a single explanatory key for any event involving any Muslim anywhere — a media reflex he calls "essentialist."

The French talk-television circuit is the most concentrated contemporary realization of this pattern. Cable news channels including CNews (owned by Vincent Bolloré, a billionaire who also owns *Le Journal du Dimanche*) and BFM TV regularly feature panels debating "the Islamic question" with the visible Muslim community largely absent from the panel itself. Zemmour rose to prominence as a commentator on these channels before launching his presidential bid — precisely the trajectory Said warned about, in which "experts" certified by repeated television appearances become the public voice on Muslim matters. The CCIE 2024 report identifies media narratives as one of the three key vectors of structural Islamophobia, alongside policy and discrimination (CCIE 2024).

Said also names the slippage between media discourse and intellectual production: alarming books by figures like Judith Miller and Bernard Lewis "feel secure until Islam puts away its sword," reinforcing public willingness to "accept the unvaryingly reductive arguments about Islam" (Said 1997, xxix). The French equivalents — Caroline Fourest's *Frère Tariq*, the corpus around the journal *Causeur*, the sustained presence of Pascal Bruckner and Alain Finkielkraut in opinion pages — perform exactly the function Said diagnoses: lending the prestige of letters to a policy environment that is otherwise embarrassed by its own inconsistencies.

Martín-Muñoz describes the same dynamic from a European vantage. Western media's treatment of the Muslim world, she writes, "constructs a media discourse at a distance: for example, it almost never portrays individuals, only masses, and usually in relation to violence or fanaticism, implicitly casting doubt on the state of civilization of these peoples. It also presents Islam as the abstract key to explain the absolute evolution of these peoples, portraying an evolving world — 'our' world — in contrast to another 'Islamic' world condemned to a repetitive cycle of hopeless misery and violence" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 26). The 2022 European Islamophobia Report describes how the French government's role consists of "mainstreaming anti-Muslim racism and renewed calls for banning of the hijab in French society" (Bridge Initiative 2023). The same report documents how the 2023 killing of Nahel Merzouk — a 17-year-old French citizen of Algerian descent shot at point-blank range by police during a traffic stop — was framed in dominant media discourse not as a case of police violence against a racialized minority but as a precipitant of "riots in the banlieues," reinforcing the Orientalist trope of the Muslim suburb as inherently unruly (Bayrakli and Hafez 2023).

This is also Mamdani's "Culture Talk" in operation: rather than analyzing the political economy of policing, of housing, of unemployment in the banlieues, French media discourse explains the events as cultural — as the inevitable expression of a Muslim disposition. "Culture Talk after 9/11," Mamdani writes, "qualified and explained the practice of 'terrorism' as 'Islamic'" (Mamdani 2004, 17); French political-media Culture Talk after Nahel qualified and explained protest as *banlieue*, which is to say as Muslim, which is to say as *outside* the legitimate citizen body. A particularly stark example came in October 2020 when, days after the murder of Samuel Paty, journalists conducting a televised report on "the rise of Islam in Roubaix" had to be placed under police protection (Times of Israel 2022). The report's premise — that the ordinary practice of Islam in a French city was itself newsworthy — illustrates how quotidian Muslim life is constituted as spectacle.

## SECTION 6 — ECONOMIC RESTRICTIONS

Economic Islamophobia in France operates through multiple channels: discriminatory hiring, housing exclusion, surveillance of Muslim financial associations, and the legal and reputational targeting of Muslim-owned businesses.

### Hiring Discrimination

The Adida-Laitin-Valfort (2010) audit study mentioned earlier found that a Muslim job applicant in France is **2.5 times less likely** to receive a callback than an otherwise-identical Christian applicant. The follow-up 2020 study (Valfort 2020) refined the finding: religious Muslim men face the largest "religiosity penalty," consistent with employer perceptions of religious Muslim men as a "risk of religious radicalism." These patterns exemplify the kind of discrimination Martín-Muñoz identifies as the foundation of structural exclusion: "anti-Muslim tendencies described in the abovementioned report are reflected through discrimination in key areas for integration such as employment, education and housing" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 23).

### Housing Discrimination

The FRA 2024 survey found that 35 percent of French Muslims could not buy or rent a house because of discrimination, with rates rising to 46 percent for Muslims with disabilities (FRA 2024). Wagner and Petev (2024) documented that following Islamist terrorist attacks, Arab/Muslim hosts on online accommodation platforms in France lose between $5.5 million and $6.2 million in revenue, suggesting collective economic punishment of innocent individuals based on ethno-religious identification — precisely the pattern Martín-Muñoz identifies, in which "national and religious origin governs control and security criteria, reinforcing the social stigmatization of Muslim communities in Western countries" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 26–27).

### Banking and Financial Surveillance

The 2021 separatism law subjects religious associations — including mosques — to mandatory government review of their bank accounts and requires declaration of all donations from abroad. Muslim charitable organizations are required to sign "contracts of republican engagement" to receive any public funding, and four organizations have had subsidies withdrawn since 2021 for alleged violations (Public Sénat 2024). The 2020 dissolution of BarakaCity, a Muslim humanitarian aid organization, was justified in part on financial grounds.

### Wage and Employment Gaps

Cesari has described the cumulative effect across Europe as the consolidation of a Muslim "underclass" — a population whose lower socioeconomic position persists "even when their levels of education or resources are comparable to other immigrant groups" (Cesari 2013b). The FRA 2024 survey found that 41 percent of EU Muslims are overqualified for their jobs, compared to 22 percent of the general population (FRA 2024). As Martín-Muñoz warns, "discrimination, ghettos and contempt for the culture and identity of a people are the perfect breeding ground for hatred and radicalisation" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 23) — a feedback loop in which structural exclusion is then cited as evidence of cultural incompatibility.

## SECTION 7 — SURVEILLANCE, EMERGENCY POWERS, AND TERRORISM

This is the section where the colonial continuity is sharpest. France's modern apparatus of Muslim surveillance descends directly from a 1955 law passed to suppress the Algerian independence movement — the *loi relative à l'état d'urgence* of 3 April 1955 was originally enacted to give French authorities exceptional powers in colonial Algeria, where it was implemented "first locally, then nationally" within days of its adoption (Thénault 2007, 63). The same legal instrument was repurposed in November 2015 after the Bataclan attacks and remained in continuous force for nearly two years.

Mahmood Mamdani's framing of modernity and violence is the indispensable lens here. He argues that the great political violence of the modern period — colonial conquest, racial pogroms, genocides — has been continuous with, not deviant from, the project of modernity itself: "Such violence gets discussed in two basic ways: in cultural terms for a premodern society and theological terms for a modern society. The cultural explanation always attributes political violence to the absence of modernity. On a world scale, it has been called 'a clash of civilizations,' as in South Asia, or 'ethnic conflict,' as in Africa" (Mamdani 2004, 4). The French security apparatus is the inverse of that pattern: the violence is administered under the banner of modernity, by a republic that locates barbarism in the population it governs. Mamdani notes that the "uniqueness of the Holocaust" sits at "the meeting point of two traditions that marked modern Western civilization: 'the anti-Semitic tradition and the tradition of genocide of colonized peoples'" (Mamdani 2004, 7, quoting Sven Lindqvist). The contemporary French state is not in either tradition's most extreme phase, but it operates on the same deep structure: the management of an internal population through legal mechanisms inherited from the colony.

### The State of Emergency, 2015–2017

Following the 13 November 2015 Paris attacks, President François Hollande declared a state of emergency that was extended six times. In its first three months alone, French authorities conducted 3,289 warrantless searches and imposed 350–400 house arrests (HRW 2016). Of those 3,289 searches, **only five** led to terrorism investigations by the Paris prosecutor's office (HRW 2016). The discriminatory pattern was unmistakable: more than 20 mosques and many Muslim associations were searched; approximately 10 mosques were closed (Amnesty International 2015).

Human Rights Watch documented the case of the Aubervilliers mosque, raided in November 2015. The president of the Association of Muslims of Aubervilliers offered the police the keys; the police broke down the doors anyway, causing extensive damage (HRW 2016). His testimony captures the lived experience of structural Islamophobia: "We feel persecuted… we end up feeling like second-class citizens" (HRW 2016). This is, almost word for word, the position Mamdani identifies as the "Bad Muslim": a political identity assigned by the state and resistant to the holder's choice. As Mamdani insists, "Judgments of 'good' and 'bad' refer to Muslim political identities, not to cultural or religious ones" (Mamdani 2004, 15) — and the Aubervilliers mosque was raided not for what its members believed but for what the state had decided their belief politically meant.

### The 2017 SILT Law: Normalizing the Exception

The October 2017 *Loi renforçant la sécurité intérieure et la lutte contre le terrorisme* (SILT) made permanent many of the most controversial state-of-emergency powers. As Amnesty International warned at the time, the bill threatened to "embed the essence of these emergency measures — intended as a temporary and exceptional response to a heightened risk — into a permanent feature of French domestic law" (Amnesty International 2017). House arrests were renamed "individual measures of administrative control and surveillance" (MICAS), and warrantless searches became "home visits" — the same powers under different names (France 24 2025).

This is the dynamic that French scholar Vanessa Codaccioni has called the "permanentisation of exceptional powers." Between 2015 and 2025 a steady stream of laws — the 2021 separatism law, the 2022 surveillance-drone law, the 2023 algorithmic video-surveillance law for the Olympics — has expanded the state's apparatus of population control, with Muslim communities as the consistent test population (France 24 2025).

### The Colonial Continuum

The 1955 emergency law was used, in 1961, to authorize the police violence in Paris that culminated in the 17 October 1961 massacre, when officers under the command of Maurice Papon killed an unknown number of Algerian protesters and threw bodies into the Seine. As Thénault (2007, 63) documents, the law's history runs "from colonial Algeria to contemporary France" — applied in Algeria in 1955, in France itself in 1958 and 1961–63, in New Caledonia in 1985, and in metropolitan France again in 2005 and 2015. The pattern reveals a law "doubly rooted in French history: as a law conceived as a response to pro-independence revolutions, but also as a law of domestic political repression" (Thénault 2007). Some scholars now describe contemporary French Muslim surveillance as a "colonial continuum" — the same legal mechanisms, the same racial logic, applied to the descendants of the same population, on the territory of the metropole rather than the colony. This is the empirical realization of Grosfoguel and Mielants' (2006) world-systems argument: the colonial racial order does not end with formal decolonization but is internalized by the metropole and applied to the postcolonial migrant body.

It is also the realization of what Mamdani calls "settler-native" violence brought home — the colonial mode of governance reapplied within the citizen body. Reflecting on Frantz Fanon, Mamdani writes that "the violence of the native is not only the injury that fuels the violence of the settler but also the fear that fuels the violence of the settler" (Mamdani 2004, 9). The contemporary French security apparatus does not face anything like the violence of the Algerian independence struggle, and yet its inherited fear — the structural disposition to read Muslim presence as latent insurrection — survives the empire that produced it. The 1955 law applied to the colony is the 2017 SILT law applied to the metropole, with the same population at the receiving end three generations later.

## SECTION 8 — COUNTER-ORGANIZATIONS AND RESISTANCE

Despite — and partly because of — escalating restrictions, French Muslim civil society and its allies have built a dense network of legal, advocacy, and grassroots organizations.

### Legal Advocacy

**Action Droits des Musulmans (ADM)** filed the September 2023 challenge to the abaya ban at the Council of State and continues to litigate discrimination cases (Al Jazeera 2023b).

**The Collectif Contre l'Islamophobie en Europe (CCIE)**, founded in Brussels in November 2020 to take over CCIF's work after the French government dissolved it, publishes detailed annual reports on Islamophobic incidents across Europe. Its 2024 report documents 1,037 incidents in France alone and offers some of the most rigorous available data on structural Islamophobia (CCIE 2024).

**The Justice and Liberties For All Committee (CJL)**, led by Yasser Louati (formerly CCIF's spokesperson), continues civil rights advocacy from inside France.

**The Ligue des Droits de l'Homme (LDH)** — France's oldest human rights organization, founded in 1898 during the Dreyfus Affair — has consistently joined Muslim civil society in litigation, including the successful 2016 challenge to the burkini bans at the Conseil d'État (Statewatch 2020).

### Sport, Culture, and Direct Action

**Les Hijabeuses** is a Paris-based collective of female footballers who wear the hijab. Founded in 2020, it brought the formal complaint against the FFF hijab ban that was rejected by the Council of State in 2023; the case is now pending before the European Court of Human Rights (Amnesty International 2024). Co-founder Founé Diawara has framed the work as a human rights struggle: "Our fight is not political or religious but centred on our human right to participate in sports" (Amnesty International 2024).

**Basket Pour Toutes** plays the same role for basketball — a coalition formed in 2022 specifically in response to the basketball federation's hijab ban.

### International Solidarity

**The European Network Against Racism (ENAR)** has coordinated international civil-society pressure on the French government, including a 2020 joint statement signed by 34 organizations across Europe condemning the dissolution of CCIF (ENAR 2020). **Amnesty International** and **Human Rights Watch** have published regular country reports documenting human rights violations affecting French Muslims (Amnesty International 2023, 2024; HRW 2016, 2025). The **UN Human Rights Committee** ruled in 2018 that France's full-face veil ban violated the human rights of those who wore the niqab (CNN 2023). The **UN Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief** has raised repeated concerns (Amnesty International 2022).

These organizations exist to do what Said insisted is the only honest alternative to the dominant pattern: replace the "sensationalism, crude xenophobia, and insensitive belligerence" of mass-media discourse with "dialogue and exchange — both of which occur in scholarly debate, in artistic production, in the encounters between ordinary human beings who do business, interact, and generally talk to" (Said 1997, xlviii). That dialogue has not yet won in France, but it has been preserved as a possibility.

### Political Voices

On the parliamentary left, **La France Insoumise** under Jean-Luc Mélenchon has been the largest political force consistently opposing the Islamophobic drift, attending the November 2019 March Against Islamophobia despite the political cost — accusations of "Islamo-leftism" (Jacobin 2025). The 2025 mass mobilization following the murder of Aboubakar Cissé in a southern French mosque drew approximately 2,000 people in La Grande-Combe and hundreds more in Paris's Place de la République (Jacobin 2025).

## SECTION 9 — SUCCESS CASES AND POSITIVE EXAMPLES

The picture is grim but not uniformly so. Muslim civil society has won significant victories.

### Conseil d'État 2016: The Burkini Wins

In August 2016, several French municipalities — Cannes, Nice, Villeneuve-Loubet, and others — adopted municipal decrees banning the "burkini" (a full-body swimsuit) on local beaches, citing public order and *laïcité*. CCIF and the Ligue des Droits de l'Homme jointly challenged the Villeneuve-Loubet decree at the Conseil d'État. On 26 August 2016, France's highest administrative court ruled the ban illegal, holding that absent demonstrated risks to public order, hygiene, or decency, municipal authorities could not restrict religious clothing on the basis of *laïcité* alone (Conseil d'État 2016, Decision No. 402742; Islamic Law Blog 2016). This ruling — although it did not extend to all bans uniformly and required individual challenges to each municipality — represented a significant judicial pushback against the misuse of secularism doctrine.

### Public Mobilization

The November 2019 March Against Islamophobia, held in response to the murderous attack on the Bayonne mosque, brought together civil society, antiracist organizations, and parts of the parliamentary left in Paris. It marked the first such mass mobilization specifically against Islamophobia and demonstrated the existence of an organized constituency rejecting structural anti-Muslim racism (Jacobin 2025).

### CCIE: Resilience Through Reconstitution

When the French government dissolved CCIF in December 2020, the organization had already transferred its assets and reconstituted as the CCIE in Brussels. The group continues its monitoring work at European scale and now reports on Islamophobic incidents across Europe (CCIE 2024). The dissolution did not silence the work — it relocated it, illustrating the limits of the French state's repressive apparatus when civil society can operate transnationally.

### International Recognition

The UN's 2022 establishment of the International Day to Combat Islamophobia (15 March) — opposed by France, India, and the EU — passed despite French opposition (Middle East Eye 2022). The annual European Islamophobia Report (now in its tenth edition in 2024) and the UN Special Rapporteur reports have created institutional documentation of French anti-Muslim policies that constrain France's ability to claim international human rights legitimacy (Bayrakli and Hafez 2024).

### Generational and Cultural Change

Despite hostile policy environments, French Muslim communities have produced a generation of civil rights lawyers, scholars, journalists, athletes, and artists who operate confidently in French public life. The Hijabeuses' challenge to the FFF ban, the public profile of legal scholars like Rim-Sarah Alouane, and the visibility of Muslim athletes like Sounkamba Sylla — even when systematically excluded — represent a form of resistance that is reshaping the cultural ground on which the next political battles will be fought.

## CONCLUSION: STRUCTURE, NOT ANOMALY

The argument of this project is that what France calls *laïcité*, when applied to its Muslim population, has come to function as something it was never meant to be: a structural mechanism for producing Muslim difference as a problem to be governed, contained, and disciplined. This is not a story about a few bad laws or a few extreme politicians. It is a story about how a centralized democratic state, drawing on Orientalist tropes Said identified, deploying the Mamdani binary at the policy level, exploiting the "unconscious Islamophobia" Martín-Muñoz names, and operating within the colonial continuum Grosfoguel describes, has constructed an entire apparatus — legal, electoral, surveillance, economic, and discursive — for the management of one community.

What makes the French case analytically important is precisely that France is not a far-right authoritarian regime. It is a founding member of the European Union, a permanent member of the UN Security Council, the country that gave the world the Declaration of the Rights of Man. The fact that this state has built such an apparatus — and built it through the legitimate mechanisms of democratic legislation — is what makes the French case a test of Bazian's structural-Islamophobia framework. The framework does not predict that Islamophobia thrives only where racism is unchecked; it predicts that Islamophobia operates as a structural organizing principle precisely within the institutions that proclaim universal values. As Martín-Muñoz observes, the European pattern is one in which "since these sentiments are expressions of patriotism and self-defence, the ensuing Islamophobic sentiments are legitimised and forgiven by society" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27).

This is also why Mamdani's insistence that "good" and "bad" Muslim are *political* designations matters so much for the French case. The state is not, in any honest sense, defending secularism against religious encroachment; it is sorting one community of citizens into the assimilable and the unassimilable, the certified and the dissoluble — the *Forum de l'Islam de France* on the one hand, the dissolved CCIF and the closed mosques on the other. "Judgments of 'good' and 'bad' refer to Muslim political identities, not to cultural or religious ones" (Mamdani 2004, 15). The French Republic is producing those identities as a condition of the very citizenship it claims to extend universally.

And this is why Said's critique of the broader Western pattern remains uncomfortably current. The French political-media-policy nexus continues to operate within the assumption that Muslims and "we" are "shut off from each other, despite literally centuries of borrowing and crossing over" (Said 1997, xxxiii) — even as more than half of French Muslims are French-born citizens whose parents and grandparents built postwar France. The denial of that "borrowing and crossing over" — the centuries of Mediterranean exchange, the colonial labor that built the metropolitan economy, the cultural and intellectual contributions of Muslim French citizens across every domain of national life — is the precondition for the laïcité apparatus to operate at all.

The hopeful counter-history is that resistance also operates structurally. Hijabeuses, Action Droits des Musulmans, CCIE, ENAR, the Ligue des Droits de l'Homme, and the broader left have built, in the same period, an integrated counter-architecture of legal, political, and cultural opposition. The 2016 burkini ruling, the UN Human Rights Committee's 2018 finding, the European Islamophobia Reports, the persistent litigation now reaching the European Court of Human Rights — these are not victories that have reversed the policy direction, but they are the sites where the next ten years of contestation will play out.

Whether *laïcité* can be reclaimed for its original 1905 meaning — protective rather than disciplinary — is the open question of contemporary French democracy.
