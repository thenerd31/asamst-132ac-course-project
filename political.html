Section 04

Political Figures & Electoral Islamophobia

Le Pen, Zemmour, Macron, Darmanin, Retailleau — and the migration of explicit anti-Muslim discourse from the xenophobic margins to the centrist mainstream.

If the legal architecture is the skeleton of structural Islamophobia, electoral politics is the muscle that drives its expansion. Three figures illustrate the continuum.

Marine Le Pen (Rassemblement National, formerly Front National)

Le Pen took over the Front National from her father Jean-Marie Le Pen in 2011 and rebranded it the Rassemblement National (RN) in 2018, pursuing a strategy of dédiabolisation — making the party's ideology palatable to mainstream voters. In substance, however, RN's anti-Muslim positioning has only intensified. In a January 2021 press conference launching the policy that anchored her 2022 presidential campaign, Le Pen called for a ban on the hijab in all public places, declaring that "the headscarf is an Islamist item of clothing" and that the broader "Islamist ideologies" she sought to outlaw were "totalitarian and murderous" (Al Jazeera 2021). She advanced to the second round and received 41.5 percent of the vote against Macron — a record for the French far right. In June 2024, RN became the largest French delegation to the European Parliament, prompting Macron to call snap legislative elections marked by what Human Rights Watch described as "increased racist and xenophobic rhetoric" (HRW 2025).

Le Pen's success illustrates the precise mechanism Martín-Muñoz identifies (2010, 27): the migration of explicit anti-Muslim discourse from the xenophobic margins to mainstream legitimacy by trading on a society-wide "unconscious Islamophobia" that has already redefined anti-Muslim measures as patriotic self-defense. The party did not need to soften its program — it needed only to frame the program in republican rather than racial terms. Laïcité did the rest of the work.

Éric Zemmour (Reconquête)

A polemicist and television commentator turned politician, Zemmour ran for the presidency in 2022 on an explicitly civilizational platform, openly endorsing the "Great Replacement" conspiracy theory — the white-nationalist claim that Europe's white Christian population is being deliberately replaced by Muslim immigrants. He has described French banlieues as sites of "jihad" and called for a Ministry of Remigration to deport Muslims en masse (INFORRM 2023). The European Court of Human Rights case Zemmour v. France (2022) records his on-camera statements that "for thirty years we've been experiencing an invasion, a colonisation, which is bringing about a conflagration" and that Muslims in France "need to be given a choice between Islam and France" (ECtHR 2022, para. 6, citing France 5 broadcast of 16 September 2016). The Cour de cassation made his conviction for incitement to discrimination and religious hatred definitive on 17 September 2019, ruling that the statements "designated all Muslims in France as invaders and ordered them to renounce their religion or leave the territory of the Republic" (Cour de cassation 2019, para. 12). He finished fourth in the 2022 first round with 7 percent of the vote — a remarkable showing for a candidate openly advocating mass deportation — and the European Court of Human Rights rejected his free-speech appeal in December 2022 (ECtHR 2022).

Zemmour is the French exemplar of what Said diagnosed in the figure of Bernard Lewis: the credentialed expert whose function is to lend intellectual respectability to the proposition that Islam is essentially incompatible with the West. Lewis, Said wrote, marshaled a distorted erudition designed "to portray the whole of Islam as basically outside the known, familiar, acceptable world that 'we' inhabit" (Said 1997, xxx). Zemmour's invocations of medieval French history and demographic data perform exactly the same operation in the French setting — and have moved the Overton window far enough that openly mass-deportationist positions are now within the mainstream electoral conversation.

Emmanuel Macron and the "Centrist" Drift

The most analytically important figure is not Le Pen or Zemmour but Emmanuel Macron, because Macron demonstrates how Islamophobic frames have migrated from the far right to the center and become governing policy. Macron campaigned in 2017 as a centrist liberal; by his second term, his interior minister Gérald Darmanin was attacking Marine Le Pen from her right, accusing her of being "too soft" on Islam (Al Jazeera 2025). In October 2020 Macron delivered a major speech announcing a campaign against "Islamist separatism," which became the framing of the 2021 law. As Al Jazeera's analysis puts it, "in the late 2010s [Islamophobia] entered the mainstream by being embraced by centrist politicians and the media" (Al Jazeera 2025).

This is precisely the dynamic Bazian (2018) identifies: the "Clash of Civilizations" frame, originally a niche position of conservative theorists like Huntington, has been adopted by the centrist political class as the operating logic of governance. It is also the institutional realization of the Mamdani binary. By creating the Forum de l'Islam de France in 2022 to construct an officially-approved "French Islam," Macron's government has effectively built an apparatus for producing what Mamdani calls "good Muslims" — those whose political identity has been certified compatible with the state (Mamdani 2004, 15) — and then dissolving the organizations of those who have not received the certification. Martín-Muñoz's gloss of the same dichotomy fits the French setting word for word: the certified "good Muslim" in the European imagination is "Westernized," "secular," and willing to "confirm our demonized stereotypes of Islam"; the rest are reclassified as "bad" by default (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 28).

Gérald Darmanin and Bruno Retailleau

Darmanin (Interior Minister 2020–2024, now Justice Minister) and his successor Bruno Retailleau (Interior Minister from 2024) have been the executive face of this turn. Darmanin ordered the dissolution of CCIF in 2020. Retailleau, in a Le Figaro interview reported by Jacobin, explicitly rejected the term "Islamophobia," claiming it has "an ideological connotation because it comes from the Muslim Brotherhood" (Jacobin 2025), and at a March 2025 rally titled "For the Republic, Against Islamism" he told attendees: "Down with the veil" (Jacobin 2025). Both are now positioning for the 2027 presidency.
