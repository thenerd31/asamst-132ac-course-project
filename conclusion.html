Conclusion

Structure, Not Anomaly

Why a founding member of the European Union, the country that gave the world the Declaration of the Rights of Man, is the analytically critical case for Bazian's structural-Islamophobia framework.

The argument of this project is that what France calls laïcité, when applied to its Muslim population, has come to function as something it was never meant to be: a structural mechanism for producing Muslim difference as a problem to be governed, contained, and disciplined. This is not a story about a few bad laws or a few extreme politicians. It is a story about how a centralized democratic state, drawing on Orientalist tropes Said identified, deploying the Mamdani binary at the policy level, exploiting the "unconscious Islamophobia" Martín-Muñoz names, and operating within the colonial continuum Grosfoguel describes, has constructed an entire apparatus (legal, electoral, surveillance, economic, and discursive) for the management of one community.

What makes the French case analytically important is precisely that France is not a far-right authoritarian regime. It is a founding member of the European Union, a permanent member of the UN Security Council, the country that gave the world the Declaration of the Rights of Man. The fact that this state has built such an apparatus, and built it through the legitimate mechanisms of democratic legislation, is what makes the French case a test of Bazian's structural-Islamophobia framework. The framework does not predict that Islamophobia thrives only where racism is unchecked; it predicts that Islamophobia operates as a structural organizing principle precisely within the institutions that proclaim universal values. As Martín-Muñoz observes, the European pattern is one in which "since these sentiments are expressions of patriotism and self-defence, the ensuing Islamophobic sentiments are legitimised and forgiven by society" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 27).

This is also why Mamdani's insistence that "good" and "bad" Muslim are political designations matters so much for the French case. The state is not, in any honest sense, defending secularism against religious encroachment; it is sorting one community of citizens into the assimilable and the unassimilable, the certified and the dissoluble: the Forum de l'Islam de France on the one hand, the dissolved CCIF and the closed mosques on the other. "Judgments of 'good' and 'bad' refer to Muslim political identities, not to cultural or religious ones" (Mamdani 2004, 15). The French Republic is producing those identities as a condition of the very citizenship it claims to extend universally.

And this is why Said's critique of the broader Western pattern remains uncomfortably current. The French political-media-policy nexus continues to operate within the assumption that Muslims and "we" are "shut off from each other, despite literally centuries of borrowing and crossing over" (Said 1997, xxxiii), even as more than half of French Muslims are French-born citizens whose parents and grandparents built postwar France. The denial of that "borrowing and crossing over" (the centuries of Mediterranean exchange, the colonial labor that built the metropolitan economy, the cultural and intellectual contributions of Muslim French citizens across every domain of national life) is the precondition for the laïcité apparatus to operate at all.

The hopeful counter-history is that resistance also operates structurally. Hijabeuses, Action Droits des Musulmans, CCIE, ENAR, the Ligue des Droits de l'Homme, and the broader left have built, in the same period, an integrated counter-architecture of legal, political, and cultural opposition. The 2016 burkini ruling, the UN Human Rights Committee's 2018 finding, the European Islamophobia Reports, the persistent litigation now reaching the European Court of Human Rights: these are not victories that have reversed the policy direction, but they are the sites where the next ten years of contestation will play out.

Whether laïcité can be reclaimed for its original 1905 meaning (protective rather than disciplinary) is the open question of contemporary French democracy.
