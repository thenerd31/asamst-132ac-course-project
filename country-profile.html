Section 01

Country Profile

Geography, demographics, the long colonial arc, and what laïcité was originally designed to do.

Geography, Population, Religious Diversity

France is a republic of approximately 68.4 million people (CIA World Factbook 2024). Officially, the French state does not collect data on religion or ethnicity in its census; the 1872 law prohibits religious or ethnic categorization, a fact that itself reflects the universalist republican ideology that shapes everything else in this report. Estimates of the Muslim population therefore rely on survey research and demographic modeling.

Pew Research Center (2017) estimated approximately 5.7 million Muslims in France (the second-largest Muslim community in Western Europe after Germany), comprising roughly 8.8 percent of the population. The 2019-2020 INSEE-INED Trajectories and Origins survey (the second of its kind) provides the most rigorous recent demographic profile of French Muslims (INSEE-INED 2023).

The community is overwhelmingly Maghrebi in origin, with the remainder drawn primarily from sub-Saharan Africa and Turkey, a distribution that mirrors France's twentieth-century colonial presence (Pew 2017; Brookings Institution 2007). Critically, more than half of French Muslims are French-born citizens: roughly 50 percent were born French, 24 percent acquired citizenship, and 26 percent are foreign nationals (Pew 2017). This distribution matters because much of French political rhetoric speaks of "Muslims" as if they were recent or foreign, when most are second- or third-generation citizens whose presence in France is a direct legacy of French colonialism.

Historical Roots: From Empire to Banlieue

Muslim presence in France is inseparable from French imperial history. France conquered Algeria in 1830, Tunisia in 1881, and Morocco in 1912; it ruled West African territories with majority-Muslim populations from the 1880s and held a mandate over Syria and Lebanon from 1920 (Brookings Institution 2007). During the First World War, approximately 500,000 African soldiers (the majority of them Muslim from the Maghreb and West Africa) fought under the French flag in mainland France; an estimated 100,000 died, including roughly 70,000 at Verdun alone (Modern Ghana / RFI 2026). The first stone of the Great Mosque of Paris was laid in 1922 and the building was inaugurated in 1926 to honor those soldiers, funded in part by the French state through the law of 19 August 1920, in a striking exception to the 1905 separation of Churches and State (Modern Ghana / RFI 2026).

The contemporary Muslim community, however, was largely formed by post-1945 labor migration. Following the Algerian War of Independence (1954-1962), France actively recruited North African workers to fill labor shortages in the industrial economy. After the 1973 oil crisis ended large-scale labor migration, family reunification became the dominant pathway, and the postwar generation gave rise to the banlieues, the often impoverished suburban housing developments where much of the French Muslim population still lives today (Brookings Institution 2007).

This colonial-to-postcolonial trajectory matters analytically. As Grosfoguel and Mielants (2006) argue from the world-systems perspective, the racial categorization of Muslims in Europe cannot be separated from the longue durée of the colonial/modern capitalist/patriarchal world-system: the same epistemic structure that legitimated French rule in Algeria continues to legitimate the marginalization of Algerians' descendants in France. Martín-Muñoz puts the longer European arc bluntly:

European colonial thought felt that it had to provide moral and ethical justification for the political domination and economic exploitation it exerted beyond its borders. This gave rise to a duality between 'civilisation' and 'barbarism,' the concept of race and the principle of European cultural superiority over 'other cultures.' (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 24)

She quotes the French statesman Jules Ferry's 1885 parliamentary speech invoking the duty "of superior races to civilize inferior races" (Martín-Muñoz 2010, 24). The vocabulary of contemporary French universalism (valeurs républicaines, intégration, vivre-ensemble) has substituted civilizational uplift for biological race, but the structural relation between metropole and (former) colonial subject is recognizably continuous. As one researcher noted regarding the 2021 separatism law:

Accusing some Muslims today of being separatists is therefore part of France's revolutionary and colonial heritage. It implies that a part of the national community behaves like an enemy within. (The Conversation 2021)

What is Laïcité?

To understand French Islamophobia, one must understand laïcité, the French principle of secularism enshrined in the law of 9 December 1905 on the Separation of the Churches and the State. The 1905 law was originally a liberal settlement of the conflict between the French Republic and the Catholic Church. It rests on three principles: independence of the state from religion, freedom of conscience, and non-discrimination based on religion (Cesari 2023).

The 1905 law, in its original form, was protective of religious minorities; it removed state coercion and guaranteed each citizen the right to practice or not practice any faith. As French legal scholar Rim-Sarah Alouane told CNN in 2023, contemporary policy "stands in opposition to the liberal core of the 1905 Law on Separation of Church & State — a law we've been distorting and weaponizing since the '90s" (CNN 2023).

This is the analytical hinge of the project: France did not invent a new anti-Muslim ideology from scratch. It took a liberal protection and inverted it into an instrument of religious surveillance, applied with disproportionate force to one community.
